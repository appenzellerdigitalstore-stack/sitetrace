What this email deliverability check does

Email deliverability is whether your messages reach the inbox — not the spam folder, not a bounce, not a silent drop. Receivers like Gmail, Outlook, and Yahoo check a chain of DNS records on every incoming message: SPF says which servers are allowed to send for you, DKIM proves the message wasn't tampered with, and DMARC tells the receiver what to do when something fails. This tool queries all five of those public records plus MX (where your incoming mail goes) in one shot and gives you a single score and a per-record diagnosis. No email is sent, no credentials are needed, and nothing leaves your browser except the domain you typed.

How email deliverability checking works

When a receiving mail server gets a message claiming to be from your domain, it looks up four public DNS records in parallel: SPF (TXT at the apex), DKIM (TXT at a selector under _domainkey), DMARC (TXT at _dmarc), and MX (where to deliver mail to you). This tool runs the same lookups your recipients run, and scores the configuration from 0 to 100 based on what's present and whether each record is configured for strict enforcement. A score of 80+ means your domain is properly authenticated; below that, the issues list tells you exactly what to add or change.

Email deliverability is whether your messages reach the inbox — not the spam folder, not bounced, not quarantined silently. Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo, and the major corporate mail servers (Mimecast, Proofpoint, Barracuda) all make this decision based on a chain of DNS records plus the IP reputation of the sending server. A misconfigured domain can deliver 100% of mail to spam even with a clean IP and a great list. Conversely, a domain with strict SPF, DKIM, and DMARC at p=reject will deliver reliably even from a new IP — receivers trust the authentication over the reputation.

SPF (Sender Policy Framework) — RFC 7208. A TXT record at the apex of your domain that lists the IP addresses and servers allowed to send mail for you. When a message arrives claiming to be from you, the receiver looks up your SPF record, walks the chain (the SPF mechanism include follows the SPF record of another domain, like Google's _spf.google.com), and checks whether the sending server's IP matches. If it doesn't, the message fails SPF. Common patterns: v=spf1 ip4:192.0.2.0/24 include:_spf.google.com -all. The -all at the end is strict — fail everything else. ~all is softfail (treat as suspect). +all allows anyone (useless, never use it).

SPF has a sharp limit: the record has to fit in a single 512-byte DNS UDP response, and each include lookup has to resolve and count toward a 10-lookup maximum. Exceed 10 lookups and your record is invalid — receivers treat the whole thing as SPA: none. The fix is to flatten the record (manually list all the IPs from your includes) or use a service like spf.wiki that auto-flattens.

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) — RFC 6376. A cryptographic signature added to every outgoing message by your mail server. The signature is over the message content + headers + a few other fields — so it both authenticates the sender (the signature can only be generated by the holder of the private key) and proves the message wasn't altered in transit. The public key is at <selector>._domainkey.yourdomain.com as a TXT record. The selector is a label your mail server chooses — common ones are default, google, k1, s1, s2, mailgun, sendgrid. Receivers look up the selector in the message's DKIM-Signature header, fetch the public key, and verify the signature.

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance) — RFC 7489. Ties SPF and DKIM together with a policy. The receiver checks both SPF and DKIM. If either passes AND the identifier (Return-Path for SPF, d= for DKIM) aligns with the From: domain, the message passes DMARC. If both fail, the policy at _dmarc.yourdomain.com tells it what to do: p=none (monitor, do nothing), p=quarantine (suspicious, send to spam), p=reject (refuse to deliver). DMARC also has a rua tag that specifies an email address to receive aggregate reports — usually a mailbox you set up specifically for reports and parse with a tool like Postmark's DMARC digester.

The right way to roll out DMARC: start at p=none with rua reports, watch the reports for 2-4 weeks to confirm all your legitimate mail passes, then move to p=quarantine and watch for false positives, then p=reject. Skipping straight to p=reject is the most common mistake — you might find that your newsletter ESP, your transactional mail provider, your customer support tool, and your corporate mail all use different DKIM selectors, and you'll break half your outgoing mail.

MX (Mail Exchange) records. Tell other servers where to deliver mail sent to your domain. example.com. 3600 IN MX 10 mail.example.com. — the 10 is the priority (lower is preferred), mail.example.com is the receiving server. For most setups you have one or two MX records pointing at your mail provider. If MX is missing or broken, mail bounces with "no mail host" — receivers can't deliver. This tool checks that MX is present and the servers respond.

BIMI (Brand Indicators for Message Identification). A newer standard. A TXT record at default._bimi.yourdomain.com pointing to your brand logo (SVG) and optionally a Verified Mark Certificate (VMC) from DigiCert or Entrust. Gmail, Yahoo, and Apple Mail render the logo next to your messages in the inbox. To use BIMI you need DMARC at p=quarantine or p=reject first. BIMI doesn't directly affect deliverability — it's a brand recognition signal — but receivers use it as another authentication check, and missing it doesn't hurt.

What receivers actually do with these. Gmail, Outlook, and Yahoo run the full chain on every message. They check SPF first (cheapest), then DKIM (one DNS lookup + one crypto verify), then DMARC (pass condition is SPF+DKIM aligned with From:). If any pass, the message is delivered to inbox subject to spam scoring. If both fail and DMARC is at p=reject, the message is refused. Then there's the spam scoring — content, IP reputation, sender history, recipient engagement. Authentication doesn't bypass it, but it's a strong positive signal.

The lookups behind this page happen in your browser via DNS-over-HTTPS. We query the apex for SPF, _dmarc for DMARC, the MX records, default._bimi for BIMI, and 20 common selectors for DKIM (default, google, k1, s1, s2, mailgun, sendgrid, postmark, mailjet, sparkpost, ses, cm, m1, m2, mx, etc.). The score is 25 each for SPF, DKIM, DMARC, MX present and 5 for BIMI if present (since BIMI is optional). If you want to do it from CI:

$ curl -X POST https://api.sitetrace.it.com/api/email-deliverability -H 'Content-Type: application/json' -d '{"domain":"example.com"}'
# → {score, spf, dkim: {selector: {pass, value}}, dmarc, mx: [...], bimi}

Or via dig: dig +short TXT example.com (SPF), dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.com (DMARC), dig +short MX example.com (MX), dig +short TXT google._domainkey.example.com (DKIM at the google selector). Same plan, same answers, your choice of interface.

Frequently asked questions

What is email deliverability?

Email deliverability is whether your messages reach the inbox — not the spam folder, not a bounce, not a silent drop. It depends on a chain of DNS records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) and the reputation of the IP addresses you send from. Receivers like Gmail, Outlook, and Yahoo check these records on every message and use them to decide whether to accept, flag, or reject the mail.

What does SPF do?

SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a DNS TXT record that lists the IP addresses and servers allowed to send mail for your domain. When a receiver gets a message claiming to be from you, it checks your SPF record. If the sending server is not on the list, the message is treated as forged. The record looks like v=spf1 ip4:192.0.2.0/24 include:_spf.google.com -all. The -all at the end means "only these sources are allowed, reject everything else" — that is the strict setting.

What does DKIM do?

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) is a cryptographic signature that proves the message was not altered in transit and was sent by a server holding the private key. The matching public key is published as a DNS TXT record at <selector>._domainkey.yourdomain.com. Common selectors include default, google, k1, s1, s2, mailgun, and sendgrid.

What does DMARC do?

DMARC ties SPF and DKIM together and tells receivers what to do when a message fails. A DMARC record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com can be set to p=none (monitor only), p=quarantine (send failing mail to spam), or p=reject (refuse failing mail outright). Without DMARC, receivers have no instruction — they may accept, flag, or reject forged mail however they like.

Why does my DKIM record not show up?

This tool probes the most common DKIM selectors (default, google, k1, s1, s2, mailgun, sendgrid, and a few others). If your mail server uses a custom selector, it will not be found. To find your actual selector, log into your mail server or ESP and look at the DKIM configuration, or send yourself a real email and inspect the DKIM-Signature header — the part before .dkim in the d= tag is your selector.

Will this tool send a real test email?

No. It only reads public DNS records (TXT and MX) for the domain you enter. It does not send mail, does not contact the mail server, and does not require any credentials.
