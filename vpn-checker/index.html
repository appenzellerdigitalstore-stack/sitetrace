How to tell if your VPN is working

When a VPN is working, the rest of the internet should see the VPN server’s IP, not yours. The VPN server’s IP belongs to a hosting provider, the city shown will be the city the VPN server is in (often very different from your real city), and the connection type will be flagged as a data center or VPN. This tool reads all of those signals from your current connection and shows them in plain English.

How VPN detection works

Every public IP belongs to an autonomous system (AS) — usually an ISP, a hosting provider, or a mobile carrier. VPN providers use hosting provider IPs because the VPN servers themselves run in data centers. This tool cross-references the IP's AS and registered organization against known VPN, proxy, and Tor exit-node lists, and flags the connection type accordingly.

VPN detection isn't magic. There are three signals that websites use to tell whether you're on a VPN, and each has well-known failure modes.

Signal 1: ASN lookup. Every public IP belongs to an autonomous system — a residential ISP like Comcast or Verizon, a hosting provider like Hetzner or AWS, or a mobile carrier. VPN providers run their servers in data centers, so the IP they hand you is almost always a hosting provider's IP. The first check is therefore "is this IP in a known hosting ASN?" — if yes, you're probably behind a VPN, a proxy, or a server. This catches 90% of VPN traffic.

Signal 2: IP-block match. Major VPN companies publish the IP ranges they lease. NordVPN, Surfshark, Mullvad, Proton, ExpressVPN, PIA — all of them have large published IP lists that update weekly. The second check is "is this IP in any of the top 150 commercial VPN providers' ranges?" — this catches most consumer VPN traffic even when the IP is technically hosted on a smaller provider that the ASN check missed. The Tor Project publishes a similar list of exit nodes every hour.

Signal 3: Reverse-DNS and threat reputation. VPN providers often set a reverse-DNS record like us-nyc-1.nordvpn.com or fr-par-relay.proton.me. Looking up the reverse DNS is a free, fast third signal — if the PTR record says it's a VPN, it's a VPN. Services like IP2Location, IPinfo, IPQualityScore, and MaxMind bundle these signals (and hundreds of others) into commercial reputation databases that websites subscribe to. The detection isn't perfect, but it's good enough for most use cases.

Where detection fails. Residential proxy networks (Bright Data, Oxylabs, Smartproxy, IPRoyal) rent real consumer ISP connections from people who have installed their software in exchange for free VPN access. The traffic emerges from a real residential IP — Comcast, Orange, Deutsche Telekom — that has no commercial relationship with any VPN provider. The detection rate for these is roughly 50%, and it depends on ASN reputation heuristics (e.g., "this residential IP was observed as a proxy exit yesterday") rather than IP-block match. If you need to defeat this kind of detection, you need a residential proxy of your own — and that costs real money.

VPN vs proxy vs Tor — what's the same, what isn't. A VPN encrypts all traffic from your device at the OS level and routes it through a tunnel to the VPN server. A proxy is per-application (browser, curl, a single game client) and usually doesn't encrypt — it just forwards your request from a different IP. HTTP proxies add X-Forwarded-For and Via headers that identify themselves. SOCKS5 proxies don't add identifying headers but also don't encrypt. Tor is a special case — three random encrypted hops, the destination only sees the exit node's IP, and the exit node operator can see plaintext for non-HTTPS connections. From a detection standpoint: VPN IPs look like datacenter IPs, proxy IPs look like datacenter IPs (or residential for residential proxies), and Tor IPs are explicitly published and flagged.

DNS leaks. Even if your VPN is up, your DNS queries might still go to your ISP's resolver — which leaks every domain you visit to your ISP. Most modern VPN clients route DNS through the tunnel to a private resolver (Mullvad uses 10.64.0.1, Proton uses 10.0.0.1, etc.). You can verify by visiting a DNS leak test page; the tool will show which resolvers answered. If your ISP's resolver is in the list, your VPN is leaking DNS.

WebRTC leaks. WebRTC (the protocol behind video calls in the browser) can leak your real local IP through STUN requests, bypassing the tunnel. Modern browsers (Firefox, Brave, Chrome with extensions) disable WebRTC by default or let you control which IPs are exposed. Most modern VPN clients also include a WebRTC-block setting. If you see a 192.168.x.x or 10.x.x.x address alongside your VPN IP in a WebRTC test, your browser is leaking your local IP.

Common VPN failure modes. The tunnel can drop mid-session if the VPN server reboots or your internet blips — most clients have a "kill switch" that blocks all traffic when the tunnel is down. Split tunneling sends only specific traffic (browser, a specific app) through the tunnel while everything else uses your real connection — handy for streaming local content, but if you forget it's on, your real IP is exposed for everything outside the tunnel. The handshake can take 5-30 seconds when you first connect; loading a page before the handshake completes shows your real IP. And of course, free VPNs often log your traffic and sell it — which is no better than having no VPN.

The detection behind this page works entirely in your browser and a Cloudflare Worker. Your browser makes a request to the Worker, the Worker fetches your public IP from the request headers (Cloudflare adds cf-connecting-ip), then forwards to IP2Location for geolocation enrichment and to a small bundled database of VPN-provider IP ranges for the connection-type classification. The whole round trip is typically 300-700ms. We don't log the IP, don't read Cloudflare's logs, and don't store anything beyond the response cache (5 minutes for IP-lookup data, 1 hour for VPN ranges).
