ip_h1 = What is my IP address?
ip_intro_h2 = About this IP checker
ip_intro_text = Your public IP address is the unique number that identifies your device on the public internet. Every site you visit, every API you call, and every email you send uses it. SiteTrace shows you the same information any service can see when you connect — your IP, approximate geographic location, internet service provider, and whether your traffic is being routed through a VPN, proxy, or Tor.
ip_affiliate_disclosure = Some links below are affiliate links. We may earn a commission at no extra cost to you.
ip_faq_h2 = Frequently asked questions
ip_faq_1_q = What is a public IP address?
ip_faq_1_a = A public IP address is a unique number assigned to your device by your internet service provider. It is how the rest of the internet identifies you and routes traffic back to you. Every website you visit sees this address, and it is the basis for approximate geolocation, blocking, and analytics.
ip_faq_2_q = How does this tool detect my IP?
ip_faq_2_a = Your browser makes a request to a public IP-geolocation service when you load the page. The remote service sees your IP (because all internet requests include the source address) and returns details about where that IP is registered, who owns it, and what type of connection it appears to be.
ip_faq_3_q = Can someone find my exact home address from my IP?
ip_faq_3_a = No. A public IP only reveals the city or region registered to your ISP, not your home address. The only parties who can map an IP to a specific physical address are your ISP and law enforcement with a court order. Public services can only see the city-level location of the IP block.
ip_faq_4_q = Does a VPN hide my IP?
ip_faq_4_a = Yes. A VPN replaces your real IP with the IP of the VPN server, so the services you connect to see the VPN server's IP instead. This tool can usually detect that a VPN is in use even when it cannot see your real IP, because VPN server ranges are well known.
ip_faq_5_q = Why does my IP address change?
ip_faq_5_a = Most residential and mobile connections use a dynamic IP, which your ISP reassigns periodically (when your router reconnects, after a few days, etc.). Static IPs are usually reserved for servers, business connections, and some power users. If your IP changes constantly you are almost certainly on a mobile or shared connection.
ip_faq_6_q = What is the difference between IPv4 and IPv6?
ip_faq_6_a = IPv4 is the older 32-bit format (e.g. 192.168.1.1, ~4.3 billion addresses). IPv6 is the newer 128-bit format (e.g. 2001:db8::1, essentially unlimited addresses). Most networks and providers support both. This tool shows you whichever your connection uses, and many modern connections have both.
ping_h1 = What is a ping test?
ping_intro_h2 = How this ping test works
ping_intro_text = A ping test measures the round-trip time it takes for a small packet of data to travel from your device to a target server and back. It is the most direct way to feel your connection's latency: lower numbers mean a snappier connection for gaming, video calls, and real-time apps. This tool pings nine major global servers every few seconds so you can see how your latency varies and which region is closest to you.
ping_faq_h2 = Frequently asked questions
ping_faq_1_q = What is a ping test?
ping_faq_1_a = A ping test sends a small network packet (an ICMP echo request) from your device to a target server and measures how long it takes to get a reply. The result, in milliseconds, is called latency. Lower latency means the connection feels faster, especially for real-time applications like gaming and video calls.
ping_faq_2_q = What is a good ping?
ping_faq_2_a = Under 20 ms is excellent (typical for local servers and fiber connections). 20-50 ms is good (typical for nearby regional servers). 50-100 ms is average and may show occasional lag. Above 100 ms will feel sluggish for real-time apps. The pings you see here are from your location to global servers, so even fast connections show higher numbers for far-away targets.
ping_faq_3_q = What is the difference between ping and latency?
ping_faq_3_a = They are essentially the same thing measured differently. Ping is the action of sending the test packet; latency is the result. The round-trip time (RTT) you see in a ping test is a measure of latency. "Ping" is also commonly used as a verb: "ping me when you're done".
ping_faq_4_q = Why is my ping so high?
ping_faq_4_a = Common causes include physical distance to the target server, congested network paths between you and that server, a slow Wi-Fi connection, background traffic on your network (streaming, downloads, other devices), or a VPN adding extra hops. If only some servers show high ping, the issue is the path; if all of them do, the issue is your local connection.
ping_faq_5_q = Can I ping a website?
ping_faq_5_a = Yes, technically. Most websites respond to ICMP pings, but many do not (cloud providers like Cloudflare, AWS, and Google block pings by default to reduce noise). This tool pings the major services that do respond so you get a meaningful number. For services that block ping, the row will show "unreachable" — that is normal and not a sign of a problem.
dns_h1 = What is a DNS lookup?
dns_intro_h2 = About DNS lookups
dns_intro_text = DNS (Domain Name System) is the internet's address book. When you type example.com into a browser, DNS translates that human-readable name into the IP address the computer actually needs to connect. This tool lets you query any domain and see its A, AAAA, MX, TXT, NS, CNAME, or SOA records — all sent over DNS-over-HTTPS for privacy.
dns_faq_h2 = Frequently asked questions
dns_faq_1_q = What is a DNS lookup?
dns_faq_1_a = A DNS lookup is a query sent to a DNS resolver asking for the records associated with a domain name. The most common query is for the A record (the IPv4 address), but DNS also stores AAAA (IPv6), MX (mail servers), TXT (verification and policy), NS (name servers), CNAME (alias), and SOA (zone authority) records.
dns_faq_2_q = What are the different DNS record types?
dns_faq_2_a = A maps a name to an IPv4 address. AAAA maps to IPv6. MX lists mail servers for the domain. TXT stores arbitrary text, commonly used for domain verification (Google, Microsoft, Facebook) and email policies like SPF/DKIM/DMARC. NS lists the authoritative name servers for the domain. CNAME creates an alias from one name to another. SOA holds administrative information about the zone.
dns_faq_3_q = Which record type should I look up?
dns_faq_3_a = For "where does this domain point," use A or AAAA. For "where does its email go," use MX. For "who runs this domain's DNS," use NS. For "is this domain verified by Google/Microsoft/etc.," use TXT. For "is this subdomain an alias of something else," use CNAME.
dns_faq_4_q = What is DNS-over-HTTPS?
dns_faq_4_a = DNS-over-HTTPS (DoH) encrypts DNS queries so that no one on the path between you and the resolver can see what domains you are looking up. Traditional DNS sends queries in cleartext, which means your ISP, coffee-shop Wi-Fi, or anyone else on the network can log every site you visit. This tool uses DoH (via Google and Cloudflare) so your lookups are private.
dns_faq_5_q = Why are my DNS records different from what I configured?
dns_faq_5_a = DNS changes take time to propagate. Most resolvers respect the TTL (time to live) on each record and refresh from the authoritative server when it expires. Typical propagation is minutes to a few hours, but in some cases it can take up to 48 hours. If you just changed a record, wait and look up again later.

status_faq_h2 = Frequently asked questions
status_faq_1_q = How do I check if a website is down for me?
status_faq_1_a = Paste the URL (e.g. https://github.com) into the search box at the top of this page and press Check. The tool will probe the site from your browser, show whether it is reachable from your network, and link you to the service's official status page if we track it.
status_faq_2_q = What is the difference between "down for me" and "down for everyone"?
status_faq_2_a = "Down for me" means your connection cannot reach the service, but the service itself is fine for everyone else — usually a problem with your network, ISP, DNS, or VPN. "Down for everyone" means the service has declared an outage or degradation on its official status page, affecting all (or many) users. The two require very different responses.
status_faq_3_q = Where does this data come from?
status_faq_3_a = Status data comes from each service's official status page, mostly hosted on Atlassian Statuspage. We poll the public summary endpoint and the incident feed. 24-hour incident timelines are derived from the incident history. We do not guess or infer outages from user reports — only declared ones.
status_faq_4_q = How often is the status updated?
status_faq_4_a = When you load the page, every service is fetched in parallel (with a small concurrency cap to respect the public API). The result you see is at most a few seconds old. There is no background polling — every refresh is fresh.
status_faq_5_q = What does "monitored" mean on a service card?
status_faq_5_a = Some services do not publish a public status page. For those, the card shows "monitored" with a link to the official site. We cannot tell you in real time whether they are down, but the link lets you check directly or jump to Downdetector for community reports.
